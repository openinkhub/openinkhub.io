EEAT stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust. It comes from Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines, where it describes what helpful, reliable content looks like. EEAT is not a single ranking factor, but many signals Google uses reflect it. This EEAT audit checklist shows how to demonstrate each part on your pages, your author profiles and across the web.

What each letter means

  • Experience: first-hand knowledge, such as having used the product or done the work.
  • Expertise: skill or knowledge in the subject, shown through training, track record or depth.
  • Authoritativeness: recognition by others as a go-to source on the topic.
  • Trust: accuracy, honesty, safety and transparency. Google calls trust the most important part.

Google explains the framework in its guide to creating helpful, reliable content.

Checklist part 1: experience

  • Does the page show real use, with your own photos, screenshots or results?
  • Also, do you describe what you learned, including problems and limits?
  • Are case studies specific, with real numbers where the client allows?
  • Finally, does the author say why they know this, in a sentence or two?

Checklist part 2: expertise

  • Is each article linked to a named author with a full bio page?
  • Does the bio list relevant experience, qualifications or projects?
  • Next, is the content accurate and deep enough for the topic?
  • For health, money or legal topics, has a qualified expert also checked it?

Checklist part 3: authoritativeness

  • Do other trusted sites mention or link to you on this topic?
  • In addition, are your team members quoted in industry media or events?
  • Do you have reviews on Google and relevant platforms?
  • Is your brand described consistently across the web? See our guide to entity SEO.

Checklist part 4: trust

  • Is it easy to find your address, phone, email and company details?
  • Then, does the site use HTTPS and avoid intrusive ads or pop-ups?
  • Are prices, terms and refund policies clear?
  • Similarly, do statistics link to original sources?
  • Are pages dated and kept up to date?
  • Overall, do you avoid claims you cannot prove?

How to run the audit

You do not need special tools for this review. A spreadsheet with one row per page and one column per checklist item is enough, so a small team can finish the first pass in an afternoon. Because the questions are plain, people outside marketing, such as sales staff or founders, can also help score pages.

Start with your highest-value pages: service pages, pricing and top articles. Then score each item as yes, partly or no, and after that group the gaps. Why EEAT matters for AI search

AI assistants also try to cite trustworthy sources. For example, they look for pages with clear authorship, accurate facts and support from other sites. Common EEAT mistakes

  • Publishing articles under "Admin" or no author at all.
  • Writing about topics you have no real experience with.
  • Hiding contact details or company information.
  • Letting outdated advice stay live for years.
