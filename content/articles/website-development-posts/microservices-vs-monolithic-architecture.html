Microservices vs monolithic architecture is a question of how you split an application. A monolith keeps everything in one codebase that is built and deployed together. It is simpler to build, test, deploy and debug. Microservices split the application into small, independent services that talk through APIs. They let large teams work and scale parts separately, but add real operational complexity. For most new products and small teams, a well-structured monolith is the better start.

Microservices vs monolithic architecture at a glance

MonolithMicroservices
CodebaseOneMany small services
DeploymentAll at onceEach service separately
ScalingWhole applicationEach service on its own
ComplexityLowerHigher: networking, monitoring, data
Team fitSmall to medium teamsMany teams working in parallel
CostLower to build and runHigher infrastructure and coordination

Scalability

Microservices let you scale only the busy part. For example, the search service of a large marketplace can run on twenty servers while billing runs on two. A monolith scales as a whole, which uses more resources but is far simpler, and it handles the traffic of most businesses comfortably. Moreover, many scaling problems come from slow database queries, which microservices do not fix.

Cost

Monoliths are cheaper. One deployment pipeline, one set of servers and simple debugging. Microservices need service discovery, API gateways, distributed tracing, more monitoring and more DevOps skill. They also need teams to agree on contracts between services. So microservices make financial sense only when the benefits, such as independent teams and targeted scaling, outweigh those costs.

Speed of development

Early on, monoliths are faster: no network calls between services and easy refactoring. As the codebase and team grow, a monolith can slow down if it becomes tangled, because every change risks breaking something else. Microservices let teams release independently, which speeds up large organisations. However, a "modular monolith", one codebase with clear internal boundaries, captures much of that benefit without the overhead.

When to choose each

  • Choose a monolith for new products, MVPs, small teams and most business web applications.
  • Choose microservices for large platforms with several teams, parts with very different scaling needs, or strict isolation requirements.
  • Evolve gradually: start modular, then extract services when a clear need appears.

Martin Fowler's essay on Monolith First makes the case for starting simple.

Common architecture mistakes

First, starting a new product with microservices because big tech companies use them. Next, splitting services that share one database, which keeps them tightly coupled. Then, too many tiny services that are hard to track. Finally, letting a monolith grow without any internal structure.

What this means for your website

Your public website, whether on WordPress, another CMS or Next.js, is usually separate from the application architecture. Keep it fast and SEO-friendly, with good Core Web Vitals, whatever happens behind it.
